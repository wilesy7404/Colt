# Wheel studs on a 2004 Nissan 350Z

Research date: 2026-10-25. Every URL below was fetched on that date and returned the text cited here.

This note is background for a team maintenance rule. It is **team policy (community research), v1, not a factory or official interval.** Nissan’s public 2004 350Z schedule does not list wheel studs. No fetched NASA or SCCA rule sets a stud-replacement life for this car. Numbers that appear below stay attached to the source that printed them. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is stated. Where no source gave a number, the item is marked unknown.

The proposed starting policy is only in [Proposed team starting point](#proposed-team-starting-point). Nothing in the source sections is a team interval.

## What sources say

### Nissan does not publish a stud life

Nissan’s 2004 350Z component list includes axle and suspension parts, brake fluid, brake pads and rotors, differential oil, engine oil, and manual transmission oil. It does not list wheel studs, lug nuts, hubs, or wheel bearings as their own line items.

- Manufacturer spec: [2004 350Z component lookup](https://maintenance-schedules.nissanusa.com/maintenance-schedules/2004/350z/components/?LocaleID=en_US&RegionID=1)

The axle-and-suspension line is an inspection interval for axles and suspension, not a stud-replacement interval. See `track-wear-items.md`.

### Lug-nut torque: one reproduced owner’s-manual figure, and several unofficial track figures

A third-party site reproduces a page from a 2004 Z33 owner’s manual, “In case of emergency,” dated in the footer `04.1.22/Z33-D/V5.0`. The reproduced warning says:

- Incorrect or improperly tightened wheel nuts can let the wheel come off.
- Do not use oil or grease on the wheel studs or nuts.
- Retighten the wheel nuts after 600 miles (1,000 km), including after a flat tire.
- Tighten to the specified torque with a torque wrench as soon as possible.
- **Wheel nut tightening torque: 80 ft-lb (108 N·m).**
- Keep the nuts at that specification. The reproduction also says it is recommended to tighten them to specification at each lubrication interval.
- The procedure on that page is: clean the wheel-to-hub face, start the nuts finger tight, tighten alternately in the illustrated 1–5 sequence, lower until the tire touches, then tighten securely in that sequence.

- Anecdotal (third-party reproduction, not a Nissan-hosted page): [carmanualsonline.info, 2004 Z33, wheel-torque search](https://www.carmanualsonline.info/nissan-350z-2004-z33-owners-manual/?srch=wheel+torque)

This research pass did not find a Nissan-hosted PDF of that page. Treat 80 ft-lb (108 N·m) as a lead to confirm in the car’s own manual, not as a verified manufacturer spec.

### What ARP publishes about the studs people install on these cars

ARP’s 2026 catalog wheel-stud table lists part **100-7716** under **Subaru BRZ & WRX (extended length with nose)**, not under a Nissan heading. The row reads: knurl diameter 0.565 in, underhead length 3.000 in, knurl length 0.270 in, nose length 0.350 in, thread M12 × 1.25. Recommended hole diameter is 0.549–0.559 in in steel or cast iron, and 0.549–0.555 in in aluminum. The catalog note says a starred drill size may be too small and may need a reamer to land inside that range.

- Manufacturer spec: [2026 ARP catalog, page 131](https://arpcatalog.com/131/)

ARP’s FAQ says to size the hole from hub material and knurl diameter, using the catalog. It also says:

- Bolts and studs can be reused if they were installed and torqued correctly and show no visible damage.
- Replace them if threads are galled or corroded.
- The 0.001 in permanent-stretch replacement rule on that page is stated for **rod bolts**, not for wheel studs.

- Manufacturer spec: [ARP fastener installation FAQ](https://www.arp-bolts.com/tech/fastener-installation-faq)

An older ARP kit URL for 100-7716 (`arp-bolts.com/kits/arpkit-detail.php?RecordID=76`) returned HTTP 404 on this pass. It is not cited.

Z1 sells “ARP extended wheel studs” for the 2003–2008 350Z and for G35 models. The product page does not print an ARP part number. Z1’s description says heat-treated 8740 chrome moly, tensile strength 190,000 psi, cadmium plated, roughly 25 mm longer than stock, bullet nose, and that open-ended or extended lug nuts are required and are not included. Z1 also says these studs may not fit aftermarket wheel hubs. On that same page, a review dated August 9, 2023 says a set ordered for a 2003 350Z “only fit the rear.” Other reviews on the page report years of track use and frequent wheel changes without a reported stud break. Those reviews are individual owners, not a life test.

- Vendor guidance (product claims) and anecdotal (reviews): [Z1 ARP extended wheel studs](https://www.z1motorsports.com/wheel-spacers-and-studs/arp/arp-extended-wheel-studs-p-5410.html)

Disagreement to keep visible: ARP’s catalog application line for 100-7716 is Subaru. Z1 sells an ARP extended stud for the 350Z without showing the part number on the fetched page. A Z1 customer reported rear-only fitment on a 2003 350Z. Tensile strength of 190,000 psi is Z1’s product text. The RallyWays article says ARP extended studs are “rated to 200,000 PSI,” and the studs pictured there are described as Subaru parts. ARP’s fetched catalog page and FAQ do not state a tensile strength for 100-7716. The FAQ’s 220,000 psi figure is for ARP2000 alloy, which is a different product discussion on that page.

### Owner reports on 350Z stud breaks

These are anecdotes, not a failure rate.

**Repeated broken studs, including a cross-threaded lug nut.** On my350z, user 3hree5ive0ero wrote on August 16, 2007, “I broke yet another wheel stud,” and published a front-and-rear replacement write-up that later adds ARP **100-7716**. In a reply, the same user said the failure that time was not a torque problem: the lug nut cross-threaded while coming off, the nut stripped and stuck, and the stud threads came off with it. Another reply on that thread (Brad350, October 27, 2008) says a lug nut seized and the stud broke while trying to free it. A reply (cubu) told the author to use a torque wrench. The write-up itself uses an impact gun, washers, and an open lug nut to **seat** the new studs, and says the impact is optional on the rear procedure. A reply (Motormouth) says they had read that hammering a stud out can damage the wheel bearing. The author doubted that a careful hammer-out would do much harm. That bearing question is unresolved in the thread.

- Anecdotal: [my350z stud replacement DIY](https://my350z.com/forum/body-interior-exterior-and-lighting-diy/294698-diy-detailed-wheel-stud-replacement-front-and-rear-arp-wheel-stud-friendly.html)

**Studs shearing while changing wheels.** User punish_her, May 23, 2009, wrote of shearing studs while changing wheels, then installing ARP studs and open-ended lug nuts. Several studs had broken at the start of a trip, and the car had sat since. The post says to pull each new stud in **before** the tire goes on, at “90–100 ft lbs,” so the installer can see that the stud head is seated. A reply (Blackbird CPV35) says cheap lug nuts used to pull the studs through damaged ARP threads. Another reply (Ataru074) says they used a fresh nut every three or four studs and had no thread damage. A short reply says “impact gun son.” So on this one thread, owners both use an impact to seat studs and warn that the nut used for that pull can destroy the new threads.

- Anecdotal: [my350z rear ARP install](https://my350z.com/forum/body-interior-exterior-and-lighting-diy/436581-diy-rear-arp-wheel-stud-instilation.html)

**The stud ring leaving the hub.** This is a hub failure that takes the studs with it, not a stud that snaps in the threads. On my350z, user commasense (September 10, 2012) described a front-left hub that came apart on track: the ring holding the wheel studs separated and left a pentagonal hole. The wheel was then held by the rotor in the caliper. The same user later said that hub had 67 track days on it. User scotts300 reported the same failure the same month. User betamotorsports wrote that on a 350Z or 370Z the front hubs are consumables for track use, that they should be crack-checked and replaced at the first sign of fatigue, and that if inspection is not done they would replace hubs and front bearings every 12 to 18 track weekends. Another participant asked what role spacers played. The fetched thread does not answer that.

- Anecdotal: [my350z, mid-Ohio crash, page 4](https://my350z.com/forum/autocross-road/384454-mid-ohio-crash-4.html)

Practice those owners actually describe, which is not the same as a life limit:

- Replace the broken stud, sometimes one inexpensive dealer stud, sometimes a full ARP install.
- ARP 100-7716 is the part number one long-running DIY names for this chassis.
- Open-ended nuts are used once the studs are longer.
- Nobody in the fetched 350Z threads states a replacement every N events, laps, or engine hours. That interval is unknown.

### Causes discussed beyond this chassis

Use these as mechanisms other people report. They are not 350Z counts.

**Heat, extra torque, and re-torque while hot.** The RallyWays author writes that track torque around 100 ft-lb, hard cornering, and re-torquing studs that are still hot elongates ordinary studs until the threads will not hold. The author says not to use impact tools on lug nuts, especially aluminum or keyed nuts, and to finger-tighten in a star pattern with the car in the air, then finish with a torque wrench in a star pattern with the car’s weight on the wheels. The author says to seat a new stud fully before any thread locker, and to re-torque after 50 to 100 miles, more than once, until the knurl settles. The same article calls wheel spacers a bad idea because they load hubs and bearings. That spacer conclusion is the author’s opinion. 350Z owners in the threads above install extended studs specifically to run spacers. Those sources disagree.

- Anecdotal: [RallyWays](https://rallyways.com/806/lug-nut-torque-of-100-ft-lbs-arp-extended-wheel-studs/)

**Friction, galling, and loss of preload.** Core4, writing about wheel-stud torque in general and not about a 350Z, says clamp load is what keeps the wheel on, and that torque is only an indirect way to get it. Their points:

- Screw-in studs have failed more often in their experience than press-in studs, because a screw-in stud has a stress concentration inside the hub.
- On aluminum wheels without steel seat inserts, galling after roughly 5–10 tightening cycles raises friction so that more torque stops adding preload. They associate that with fatigue failure.
- They say 12 mm ARP and MSI studs (class 12.9) can exceed 15,000 lb of preload per stud, and that a typical tapered seat on an aluminum wheel has trouble reaching that, galled or not.
- If galling starts, clean it, or use a small amount of anti-seize, then clean and reapply after about 10 wheel changes. This conflicts with the reproduced Nissan warning not to put oil or grease on studs or nuts. Core4 is talking about the nut-to-wheel seat, not about lubricating the stud threads. The two instructions are easy to mix up. They are not the same operation, and they come from different sources.
- Dry-lube coatings on MSI nuts are, in Core4’s testing, nearly gone after about 10 torque cycles. Core4 says not to keep using the 70–75 ft-lb (12 mm) or 85–90 ft-lb (14 mm) coated-nut recommendations after that.
- After new wheels, rotors, spacers, studs, hubs, or lug nuts, Core4 says a torque check after 30–60 minutes of running is mandatory, because embedment of a few thousandths of an inch can remove meaningful preload.
- Paint on the nut seat, rotor face, or center bore can squeeze out and unload the stud. Core4 says to remove it down to bare aluminum on the nut seats.
- For a race, Core4 says loss of clamp from heat, vibration, or a low starting preload is the larger risk, and that if heat is a concern, lower the torque 10 percent and check, rather than torquing the nuts hot.

- Vendor guidance: [Core4 wheel-stud torque and friction](https://www.core4motorsports.com/tech)

The 10 percent reduction is Core4’s endurance suggestion. It is not a 350Z figure, and applying it to 80 ft-lb would invent a third torque. The v1 policy does not adopt it.

### Sanctioning-body rules

#### NASA Club Codes and Regulations, 2025.4 edition

Fetched PDF: [2025.4 NASA CCR](https://nasa-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/document/document/282/2025.4_NASACCR.pdf). Sanctioning-body rule.

HPDE vehicle rules, section 11.4.2, Wheels and Tires: the tire and rim must be in good condition, the wheel must not be cracked or otherwise damaged, the tire must not show cords, bubbles, or other visible damage, and **all lug nuts must be present and tightly hold the wheel to the hub.**

Section 11.4.3: front and rear wheel bearings should be tight and play-free. That is a bearing rule, not a stud-life rule. It is repeated in `track-wear-items.md`.

Section 15 is the racing safety-equipment chapter. In this edition, section 15.18 is **Engine Coolant**. There is no section 15.18 titled “Lug Nuts,” and the chapter does not set stud length, nut style, or a replacement interval.

Section 16.3 says drivers are responsible for inspecting their own cars for each event. It also says on-track mechanical failures of parts or systems that are the competitor’s responsibility, as defined by the Annual Race Car Technical Form, draw a fine. That is a competitor-responsibility rule, not a stud interval.

A 2026 CCR file was not retrieved. NASA Spec3’s 2026 rules say competitors follow the CCR in effect on the date of the event. Re-download the current CCR before relying on a section number.

#### The “4.5 threads / double-nut” post is not a current CCR rule

On April 1, 2009, a post in NASA’s American Iron forum quoted a supposed new CCR 15.18 requiring open lug nuts, enough exposed thread to double-nut, a minimum of 4.5 threads, with aircraft lock nuts waiving the double nut. Replies called it an April Fools joke. The author wrote “Dang. Thought I would at least fool some of you,” and a later reply says “FYI - It’s a joke.” The current 2025.4 CCR text above does not contain that rule.

- Anecdotal: [NASA forum, April 1, 2009](https://community.drivenasa.com/topic/22557-important-note-lug-and-stud-issues/)

#### NASA Spec3, 2026 rules — a different car, and the only stud-life sentence found

Fetched PDF: [2026 Spec3 rules](https://nasa-assets.s3.amazonaws.com/document/document/24109/2026_Spec3_Rules.pdf). Sanctioning-body rule for **Spec3 only**.

Section 6.3 limits Spec3 to specified BMW E36 cars (1993–1995 325i/325is, with a limited chassis allowance). It is not a 350Z class.

Section 7.7, Lug Nuts:

- 7.7.1 Lug bolts may be upgraded to threaded wheel studs, and that upgrade is highly recommended. **It is recommended that threaded wheel studs be replaced every two years.**
- 7.7.2 Lug bolts may be upgraded to press-in wheel studs, and that upgrade is highly recommended. Hubs may be machined only to accept press-in studs.

The two-year sentence is attached to threaded (screw-in) studs. The press-in sentence does not repeat it. Spec3 also says its own regulations supersede the CCR when they conflict, and that cars must otherwise meet the CCR. Nothing in the fetched Spec3 text makes this interval apply to a 350Z.

#### SCCA General Competition Rules

Edition read: [February 2026 GCR (updated through Technical Bulletin 26-02)](https://cdn.connectsites.net/user_files/scca/downloads/000/078/671/FEB-26.pdf?1771351149=). Sanctioning-body rule.

In the general technical specifications of that edition:

- 9.3.46 Suspension and steering shall be of suitable design and in good working order.
- 9.3.47 Tires shall be 124 (“U”) mph rated or better unless otherwise specified. Several production-based categories require a DOT tire of that rating. The section regulates tires, not studs.

A text search of that file for wheel-stud and lug-nut rules did not find a general stud-life, open-nut, or thread-exposure rule. One class-specific example that does **not** apply to a 350Z: Formula First requires four lug bolts or studs with lug nuts and prohibits centerlocks. A 350Z entered in a particular SCCA class still has to meet that class’s specification line. This pass did not read every spec line.

The February file is the edition whose text was read. The GCR itself says the latest electronic edition on the SCCA site supersedes earlier ones. Check the edition in force for the event.

### Where the sources disagree

1. **Torque.** Reproduced 2004 manual: 80 ft-lb (108 N·m), no oil or grease. RallyWays: about 100 ft-lb on track is common, and the author also says 20 ft-lb or more over OEM is acceptable. A 350Z owner: 90–100 ft-lb to seat the stud in the hub. Core4: do not keep using 70–75 ft-lb on coated 12 mm MSI nuts after the coating wears, and consider 10 percent less torque if an endurance race will heat the stud. ARP: no 350Z lug torque on the pages fetched. These are not interchangeable.
2. **Lubricant on the fastener.** Reproduced Nissan text: no oil or grease on studs or nuts. ARP: Ultra-Torque on the threads and nut bearing face for the torques ARP publishes, and not mixed with Loctite. Core4: a small amount of anti-seize on a galled aluminum nut seat. Different surfaces, different sources.
3. **Impact tools.** RallyWays says not to impact lug nuts. my350z write-ups use an impact to draw a stud into the hub, and a reply says cheap nuts used that way ruined ARP threads. Another reply says to use a torque wrench on the wheel nuts. Seating a stud and clamping a wheel are different steps.
4. **How many studs to replace.** Fetched 350Z write-ups often replace the one broken stud. ARP sells the extended part as a five-pack in the catalog row for 100-7716. Z1 sells the extended stud in quantities of 5, 10, 15, or 20. Neither ARP nor the forum posts say “replace the other four on that hub because one broke.”
5. **Life.** ARP: reuse if undamaged and correctly torqued. Spec3: two-year recommendation for threaded studs on a BMW class. 350Z anecdotes: no event or hour interval. One front hub lasted 67 track days; another writer suggested replacing front hubs every 12 to 18 track weekends. Those hub numbers are not stud numbers.
6. **Spacers and extended studs.** Z1 and my350z owners use longer studs so spacers and thicker wheels can be clamped. RallyWays says spacers overload hubs and bearings. The my350z hub-failure thread raises spacers as a question and does not answer it.
7. **Which ARP part is the 350Z part.** Catalog: 100-7716 is a Subaru BRZ/WRX extended stud, M12 × 1.25. A my350z DIY names 100-7716 for a 350Z. Z1 sells an ARP extended stud for the 350Z and does not print the part number; one buyer said the set fit only the rear.
8. **Tensile strength.** Z1: 190,000 psi. RallyWays: 200,000 psi. ARP catalog page fetched: no tensile figure for that stud.

## Proposed team starting point

**team policy (community research), v1, not a factory or official interval.**

This is a starting rule so the car is not managed as if studs last the life of the hub. It will be rewritten when the failure log has real dates, laps, and hours. It does not certify the car. The driver still has to meet the NASA or SCCA rules for the event actually entered.

### Hardware

- Track studs as sealed sets of five. One kit identity per corner. Do not mix studs from two kits on the same hub.
- If one stud on a corner is broken, necked, cross-threaded, galled, corroded, or was used as the nut that drew a new stud into the hub, remove that corner’s set and install a new five-stud kit. Do not put a single new stud into a used set. Owners on my350z often changed only the broken stud. v1 does not follow that practice, because the other four studs on that hub saw the same nuts, wheels, and torque method.
- The other three corners stay in service if their inspection is clean. Replacing all four corners after one failure is an allowed stricter choice. It is not required by any source found here.
- Extended studs require open-end nuts. Match the nut seat to the wheel. Z1’s 60 degree open nuts are, by Z1’s own text, the wrong nut for a factory shank-style Nissan wheel.
- Before buying, match the ARP part number to ARP’s current catalog application line and to a trial fit on each corner. Do not assume 100-7716 is correct for both axles because a forum DIY or a retailer title says so. Z1’s “may not fit aftermarket hubs” note applies if the hub is not the stock forging.
- Record spacer thickness and part number with the stud kit. Sources disagree on whether spacers are acceptable. v1 does not ban them. It does require the nut to be the type that seats on that wheel, and it requires the engagement to be enough that the nut is fully on the threads. No fetched 350Z or current NASA/SCCA rule gives a minimum exposed-thread count. Do not use 4.5 threads. That figure is from the April 1, 2009 joke post.

### Torque method

- Clamp the wheel with a torque wrench. Do not use an impact gun for the torque that holds the wheel on.
- Do not set a team torque of 90, 100, or “20 over OEM.” Those numbers are anecdotal, and they disagree.
- For stock studs and the matching stock nuts, the only candidate figure found is **80 ft-lb (108 N·m)** in a third-party reproduction of the 2004 manual, with no oil or grease on the studs or nuts, in the manual’s 1–5 sequence, and with a recheck after 600 miles (1,000 km). Confirm that figure and the sequence diagram in the manual that belongs to this car before anyone uses it. Until that confirmation, the approved torque in the maintenance system stays blank, not 80.
- For ARP or any other non-stock stud, the approved lug torque also stays blank until ARP, or the nut maker, supplies a written value for that exact stud, nut, lubricant, and wheel. The reproduced Nissan figure is not an ARP figure.
- Pull a new press-in stud in until the head is seated, using washers and a nut that will be thrown away. Do not reuse that nut on the car. Do not treat 90–100 ft-lb as the specification for that pull.
- Set lug torque cold, before the session. After a session, recheck only once the corner has cooled, and write down that the check was cold. Do not add torque on a hot stud as the way to “make sure.”
- After a new stud, nut, wheel, rotor, spacer, or hub, check torque again after 30–60 minutes of running. That check is Core4’s vendor guidance for embedment. It does not change the torque value.

### Inspection

Before the first session of an event, and any time the wheel is off:

- Five nuts present, seated, and matching the wheel (NASA CCR 11.4.2; SCCA Time Trials safety rules).
- No cracked wheel (same rules).
- No galled, necked, or corroded stud threads (ARP reuse condition).
- No galling on an aluminum nut seat (Core4).
- Hub face that carries the studs is intact. On this chassis, owners have had that ring separate from the hub. See `track-wear-items.md`.
- Bearings play-free (NASA CCR 11.4.3; SCCA Time Trials).

A stud that fails any of those checks comes off with the rest of its five-stud kit.

### Life-limit options

No fetched source gives a 350Z stud life in laps, engine hours, or events. Do not enter a lap or hour interval until the failure log supports one.

**Option A — condition only.** Follow ARP’s reuse statement: if the set was installed and torqued correctly and shows no galling or corrosion, it stays. Replace the corner set when inspection fails. No calendar cap. This matches the only wheel-stud reuse statement ARP’s FAQ makes. It does not account for heat cycles the FAQ does not discuss.

**Option B — condition plus a two-year cap. This is the v1 starting choice.** Everything in Option A, plus: replace each corner’s five-stud kit at two years from the install date, even if it looks fine. The two years come from NASA Spec3 section 7.7.1, which **recommends** replacement of **threaded** studs every two years on a BMW E36 class. It is not a 350Z rule, it is not mandatory even in Spec3, and Spec3 does not attach it to press-in studs. v1 borrows it as a calendar backstop so a set cannot stay on the car forever while the team collects failures. Label the maintenance-system rule with this source and this limitation. Do not label it as Nissan or as a NASA CCR requirement.

**Option C — replace every N events or hours.** Not available. N is unknown. Do not invent it. Revisit after several recorded failures or after a season of clean inspections, whichever comes first.

v1 uses Option B, the whole-corner replacement rule, the blank torque until the manual or ARP confirms a number, and cold torque-wrench installation.

## What to record on each stud failure

Record these before the broken parts are discarded. Missing fields stay blank. A blank is not a guess.

| Field | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Position | Corner (LF, RF, LR, RR) and stud number. Use the 1–5 sequence from this car’s owner’s manual if the diagram is in hand. If it is not, number the studs once, in writing, as viewed from outside the wheel, and keep that map. |
| Stud set or kit ID | The sealed-kit identity installed on that corner, plus the part number printed on the package. |
| Laps | Odometer of laps on that kit at the failure, and laps on the car if the kit total is unknown. |
| Engine hours | Hour reading at the failure. Do not convert hours to laps. |
| Event | Event name, date, series (NASA, SCCA, or test), and session. |
| Torque method | Torque value if one was used, wrench or impact, cold or hot, lubricant or anti-seize if any, and whether the nut was the one that seated the stud. |
| Wheel and nut type | Wheel maker and part, nut maker and part, seat type (shank, 60 degree taper, or other), open or closed end, and spacer part and thickness if a spacer was installed. |
| Photos | Stud, nut, wheel seat, and hub face, before cleanup if that is safe. |
| Notes | How it was found (noise, loose wheel, snapped while removing a nut, found at inspection), and whether the other studs on that corner looked damaged. |

Also keep the removed kit identity in the history. Do not reuse those five studs as a new kit.
